## 3d Histology of the brain
The ability to visualize and analyze `whole organs` in their `native 3D context`, with `high resolution`, offers unprecedented opportunities for studying complex structures such as the brain and understanding the intricate relationships between cells and tissues at an organ-wide scale. The increasing availability of publicly accessible 3D histology datasets is a crucial driver of progress in this field, fostering collaboration among researchers worldwide and accelerating the pace of scientific discovery.

For the study of the developing human brain, three-dimensional (3D) histology offers significant advantages over traditional two-dimensional (2D) methods. 3D reconstruction of serial histological sections enables a volumetric view of the fetal brain, allowing visualization in all three planes akin to MRI, previously unachievable with histological datasets from the fetal brain. 

This allowed qualitative assessment of the growth of brain regions and layers throughout the second trimester. This dataset represents a standardized high-resolution longitudinal snapshot of human brain development in the second trimester, with five fetal brains digitized, standardized, expert annotated, 3d-reconstructed, andvisualized in high resolution 2d and 3d, all made available online.

This volumetric perspective allows for a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of the growth and development of different brain regions and their layers throughout gestation, which is particularly crucial during the rapidly changing second trimester. Unlike 2D slices, 3D histology captures the spatial context necessary to understand the complex organization of neural circuits and to study developmental processes that occur across multiple planes. Furthermore, the detailed cellular resolution provided by histology, when extended to three dimensions, enables the investigation of microstructural changes and the identification of cytoarchitectural boundaries with greater precision than macroscopic imaging techniques. The ability to explore the developing brain in 3D through resources like DHARANI provides an invaluable tool for advancing our understanding of human neurodevelopment.

## The developing brain
 the DHARANI resource distinguishes itself by providing a longitudinal perspective on brain development during the second trimester, encompassing data from 14 to 24 gestational weeks (GW). This range is critical as it covers a period of rapid and significant neurodevelopmental changes, including the formation and maturation of key brain structures and the organization of cortical layers. By offering 3D histological reconstructions across these gestational weeks, DHARANI enables researchers to make `direct comparative observations` of how brain regions grow, how connections form, and how cytoarchitecture evolves over time. This longitudinal sampling is essential for understanding the typical trajectory of brain development and for identifying potential deviations that may arise from various factors or conditions during this sensitive period.
 
## Dharani online platform
Dharani's online platform is dedicated to providing three-dimensional (3D) histological reconstructions of the developing human brain during the critical second trimester of gestation, specifically spanning from 14 to 24 gestational weeks (GW). This unique resource encompasses five fetal brains and offers a wealth of detailed anatomical information. The platform features over 5000 histological sections, stained with both Nissl and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), at a resolution of 20 µm for coronal and sagittal sections. 

In addition to these high-resolution histological data, DHARANI also includes `postmortem MRI` scans and a comprehensive `neuroanatomical atlas` containing 466 annotated sections that cover approximately 500 distinct brain structures. The pixel resolution of the annotated histological sections is remarkably high, rendered at 1 µm per pixel. A key advancement offered by DHARANI is the 3D reconstruction of these histological sections, which enables a volumetric view of the developing fetal brain, allowing for visualization in all three anatomical planes—axial, coronal, and sagittal—much like MRI.

## Use cases
**Neuroscience**

*Neurobiologists, neuroanatomists, developmental biologists*: 
Charting the `spatial and temporal progression` of brain region formation, neuronal migration, and structural differentiation during fetal development.

Neuroanatomists can correlate the known/reported patterns and descriptions to visual spatial localization using this dataset.

*Atlas guidance*: Provides a `reference framework` for annotating new neuroimaging or histological datasets with high anatomical precision.


**Computer vision**

The DHARANI data is annotated (contains detailed region delineations in the developing human fetal brain), and can be used to train models for `segmentation, visual recognition, reconstruction, computing intermediate stages and eliciting developmental patterns` required for neurobiologists to study changes, abnormalities, events and trends.

**Education**

The data can power `interactive visualizations` and 3D models for teaching human neurodevelopment with anatomical accuracy.
